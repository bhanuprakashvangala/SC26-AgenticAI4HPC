\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results relocate the binding constraint on agent-generated parallel code
\emph{twice}. We expected it to lie in \emph{parallelization}---races and
order-dependence that a single run hides. For \Nmodels{} models on standard OpenMP
tasks it did not: the models parallelized safely, and where they failed the error
was \emph{algorithmic} and visible on the very first thread. But the deeper
relocation is the one the performance axis reveals: once correctness is largely
solved, the constraint moves to \emph{parallelism itself}. The programs the
verifier accepts span $0.33\times$ to $7.9\times$ speedup
(Section~\ref{sec:scaling}); a robustness metric defined on outputs cannot see the
difference, because it is maximized by serial code. Correctness stopped being the
hard part, and an acceptance test that scores only outputs is now measuring the
wrong axis.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{The repair loop, not the verifier's granularity, was the active
ingredient.} Holding the model, prompt, and repair budget fixed and varying only
the verification tool moves robust correctness from \Lzerorate{} (no tool) to
\Lonerate{} once a repair loop is present---and then \emph{not at all}: the
differential verifier reaches the same \Ltworate{}
(\(\Delta_{\text{L2--L1}}=\LtwoOverLone{}\)), agreeing with the single-thread check
on every one of the \Npairs{} paired cells. The gain over single-shot generation is
the loop's, not the verifier's. This does not contradict the design argument that a
single-configuration check is \emph{structurally} blind to nondeterministic
races---that remains true---but it shows the argument is only \emph{actionable}
when models actually emit such races, which here they did not.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{When differential verification pays off.}
The verifier is cheap---compile once, run a handful of thread counts with a few
repetitions against a serial reference---so its cost is a low, fixed premium. What
our study measures is the \emph{payout}: zero, when the model$\times$task
distribution produces no silent races. The payout is not zero in general---our
injected-race control (Table~\ref{tab:injected}) confirms the verifier rejects
every racy program the single-thread check accepts. It grows
with the race-proneness of that distribution: weaker or non-code-specialized models
that reach for manual shared-state updates instead of reduction clauses; tasks
whose natural parallelization invites a race rather than a library idiom; and the
distributed-memory (MPI) and GPU (CUDA) regimes our harness supports but we did not
stress here. The right way to spend the premium is to \emph{measure} it: run the
differential gate, watch how often L1 and L2 diverge for your models and tasks, and
keep the gate where they do.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{From gate to reward: a two-axis verification-guided objective.}
The acceptance gate an agent applies is also the reward a verifiable-reward RL loop would
optimize~\cite{rlvr,grpo}, and this is where the gameability becomes concrete rather than
theoretical. Treating the set of accepted programs for a task as the candidates a
generator (or a best-of-$N$/GRPO selection) chooses among, a correctness-only reward is
\emph{indifferent}: every robust program scores identically, so selection realizes only
the mean of the pool, whereas a two-axis reward (correctness times realized efficiency)
selects the fastest. On the \NmultiTask{} tasks with more than one accepted program, this
gap is not small: a correctness-only reward realizes a mean \RewardRandom{}$\times$
8-thread speedup against the two-axis reward's \RewardTwoAxis{}$\times$
(\RewardGainPct{}\% more), and on the most dispersed task it is the difference between a
\RewardSpreadLo{}$\times$ and a \RewardSpreadHi{}$\times$ program the correctness reward
rates equally. The constructive consequence, which we frame as future
work, is a \emph{second verification gate}: a performance gate whose termination
condition is a self-speedup target and which, crucially, is guarded by the correctness
gate---every optimization must remain robust---so that optimizing the two-axis reward can
never be satisfied by fast-but-wrong code. This is the natural home for the negative
result: once correctness saturates, the verification-guided objective should carry the
second axis. We distinguish this from prior performance-rewarded HPC code
generation~\cite{pareval}, which optimizes speed directly; our point is narrower and about
the \emph{reward's specification}---a single-axis correctness reward is gameable by
construction, and we measure what that costs.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{Threats to validity.}
\emph{Construct.} We study OpenMP shared-memory parallelism over
ParEval's tasks and serial references; distributed-memory (MPI) and GPU (CUDA)
hazards, though supported by our harness, are left for future work, and the hazard
taxonomy is not exhaustive. Our primary race evidence is
\emph{output-differential}, but we do not rely on it alone: we cross-checked every
accepted program with a happens-before detector (Archer/ThreadSanitizer with OMPT
annotations~\cite{archer,tsan}), which agreed---\NsanRaces{} races in the
\NsanTimed{} certified programs---while correctly flagging our injected and
open-model races, so the null is corroborated by an independent instrument rather
than an artifact of detector power. We still report it conservatively as a bound:
with $0$ observed, the silent-failure risk is under $\approx\!5\%$ at 95\%
confidence (rule of three). The performance finding does not share this
fragility---wall-time is directly measured.
\emph{Internal.} Each task has a single serial reference; because it is
sequential, it is immune to the parallel hazards under test, but it fixes the
task's intended semantics. The developer-default configuration ($\tau{=}1$) is a
modeling choice for $\mathcal{S}$, chosen as the most failure-masking single run
so that $\Delta$ upper-bounds what a lucky local check could hide.
\emph{External.} Our \Nmodels{} models are drawn from a single provider
(general, preceding-generation, code-specialized, and higher-reasoning tiers) over
\Ntasks{} tasks; holding capability high sharpens
the ablation but makes the near-absence of races unsurprising, and neither the
null nor the point estimates need transfer to other vendors, weaker models, larger
task sets, or prompt/temperature regimes. That the null held even for a
code-specialized model tempers, but does not remove, this threat. Because our
harness reaches any model
through one uniform API, cross-vendor and weaker-model replication is the most
immediate extension. We report Wilson intervals and read point estimates alongside
them rather than as precise rankings; with \Nmodels{}$\times$\Ntasks{} cells the
intervals are wide.

\vspace{3pt}
\noindent\textbf{Recommendations.}
For teams adopting agentic assistants for HPC software, a repair loop is clearly
worth having: it supplied the only improvement we measured. A differential-
verification gate---validate across thread counts and repeated runs against a
trusted reference, rather than trusting a single successful execution---is cheap
insurance we still recommend, but with eyes open: for capable models on idiomatic
tasks its marginal value over a single-thread check may be nil, and the residual
failures are algorithmic errors that call for better generation or stronger repair,
not finer verification. Treat the gate as instrumentation as much as protection:
it is also how you \emph{learn} whether your models write racy code.
